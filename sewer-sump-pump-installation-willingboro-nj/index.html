Sewer Sump Pump Installation in Willingboro, NJ

Sewer sump pump installation usually means a sewage ejector for a basement bathroom or laundry. Here is how it differs from a sump pump and how we install one.

By the Willingboro 24/7 Plumbing team. Updated 2026. 6 minute read.

Talk to a plumber
(609) 547-1417
Call Now

Sewer sump pump installation is the common phrase for adding a sewage ejector pump, which lifts toilet, shower and laundry waste from a basement fixture up to the main drain. A true sump pump is a different machine. It moves groundwater out of a pit and does not handle sewage.

Getting the two mixed up leads to flooded floors and failed inspections. We explain the difference below and how a proper install is done.

Know the difference

Sump pump or sewage ejector

Two pumps, two jobs
Sump pumpSewage ejector
Moves.Groundwater and stormwater from a pit.Toilet, sink, shower and laundry waste.
Basin.Open or covered pit near the footing drain.Sealed, gasketed basin with a vent.
Handles solids.No.Yes, up to a rated size.
Discharge.Outdoors, away from the foundation.Into the building sewer line.

Many municipalities restrict sending groundwater into the sanitary sewer, so ask before you tie a sump pump into one. Our sump pump installation and repair service covers the groundwater side, and an ejector belongs with the sewage side.

When a house needs an ejector

Drains work by gravity. If a fixture sits lower than the sewer line leaving the house, wastewater cannot flow down to it, so it collects in a basin and a pump lifts it. Basement bathrooms, wet bars and laundry sinks are typical cases.

A plumber measures the fixture drain height against the building sewer before anything is bought. A fixture on a floor above the sewer line often needs no pump at all.

Step by step

How a sewage ejector is installed

    We set the basin location, vent route and discharge path before cutting anything.

  2. 2

    If the basement floor is concrete, we saw a pit large enough for the basin.

  3. 3

    Set the basin

    The basin sits level, with a gasketed lid and fittings sealed against gas.

  4. 4

    Pipe and vent

    Fixture drains enter the basin, a vent runs to the roof or an approved connection, and a check valve and shutoff go on the discharge.

  5. 5

    Wire and test

    The pump needs a suitable electrical circuit, and we test the float and run water through to confirm.

The discharge line rises and joins the building sewer. If the sewer is old or partly blocked, the pump will not fix that, so we often suggest a camera look at the line first. Our sewer line repair page explains what that finds.

Plan on a day or more when the floor must be cut, since patching concrete adds drying time. This is not a casual weekend project. The work combines sewer gas sealing, venting, electrical wiring and a possible permit, and a leak at the lid or the check valve is unpleasant to find later.

Choosing parts

Sizing the basin and pump

The basin has to hold enough waste that the pump does not start every few seconds. A short-cycling pump wears out early, so a basin sized for the number of fixtures matters more than a bigger motor. Look for a pump rated for solids, commonly up to 2 inches, and a cast or sturdy housing.

  • A check valve that stops waste from flowing back into the basin.
  • A gate or ball valve so the pump can be serviced without draining the line.
  • A float switch with room to move, clear of the basin wall.
  • A high-water alarm that sounds before the basin overflows.

Pump runs constantly

A stuck float or failed check valve is a common cause.

Pump hums but nothing moves

The motor may be jammed or the impeller blocked.

Sewer smell near the basin

The lid gasket or vent may have failed.

Slow draining basement fixtures

The basin may be near full because the pump is not starting.

Do not flush wipes, feminine products or kitchen grease into a basement bathroom. Ejector pumps are built for human waste and paper, and anything else can wrap the impeller. A pump that is worked hard by rags is the most common reason we see for early failure.

Willingboro, NJ: permits, inspections and the sewer system

Permit rules vary, so ask the Willingboro building department. The Construction Official's office at 1 Rev. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Drive, 609-877-2200, enforces the New Jersey Uniform Construction Code, reviews permit applications and links a permit checklist and a required inspections list. Westampton and Mount Holly are separate towns with their own offices, and neighbors there should check with their own township rather than assume Willingboro rules apply.

FAQ

Questions from Willingboro homeowners

How long does a sewage ejector pump last?

Often around 7 to 10 years, depending on use and what goes down the drain. Wipes and grease shorten that, and a yearly test by pouring water into the basin shows whether the float and pump still work.

Does the pump need a vent?

Yes. The basin must be vented so gas leaves safely and the pump can move waste. We route the vent as part of the install.

Can a sump pump handle a toilet?

No. A sump pump is not built for solids or sealed against sewer gas. A toilet below the sewer line needs an ejector.

What happens during a power cut?

The pump stops, so the basin fills. A battery backup or a high-water alarm helps, and we can discuss options.

Does a finished basement bathroom in Willingboro need an ejector pump?

It depends on whether the fixture sits below the sewer line, and a plumber checks that. Ask the Willingboro building department about permits before starting.

If you live there, confirm your township's permit rules before you call, and have your fixture list ready.
